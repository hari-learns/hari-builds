How much does sheet metal laser cutting cost?

Metco does not publish a price list, and this page does not invent one. A price comes from the drawing, the material and thickness, the quantity and the finish. Here is what moves it, using only Metco’s own pages and what its customers have said, and how to get a real number quickly.

The short answer

There is no flat rate per cut or per kilo on Metco’s site. The contact page offers a free quote by phone, email or form, and a consultation where the team looks at “concepts, plans, cost saving initiatives, safety in design, prototypes and pricing”.

What Metco does state is how its set-up keeps cost down, what it can cut, and how its customers got costs out of their own parts. That is the material for the six factors below.

Six things that move the price

  1. 01

    Setup time, and how busy the laser is

    Metco’s automated 24/7 production system pairs a TruStore 3030 tower, holding up to 51 pallets of 3 tonnes each, with a Lift Master Compact that moves sheets to twin lasers. Metco says this cuts setup to 22 seconds per sheet, against 1.5 minutes without automation, and keeps the lasers busy 85 to 90 percent of the time. Its stated result: “Normal industry costs can be cut by 60%.”

    Metco’s own claim, from its laser cutting page. Read the source page

  2. 02

    Material and thickness

    These are the first two numbers the team needs, because they decide which machine the job takes. The laser has published limits per material (table below). Dyno’s account shows the cost side: strength came from adding a fold instead of using thicker plate, and the whole assembly was standardised on 3 mm steel “to avoid waste”.

    Laser limits: laser cutting page. Dyno: case study. Read the source page

  3. 03

    Quantity, and what each setup has to pay for

    Pertronic’s production manager recalls being told by suppliers: “your parts require a lot of production setup for the small quantities you order, this makes the parts expensive.” Metco’s answer was to redesign the parts. Metco says its customers range from one-off commissions to runs of tens of thousands. Designtree praises a “no job too small” company that will do 10 to 20 items at a time, and the New Zealand Defence Force says Metco puts “no restrictions on minimum quantities”.

    Pertronic, Designtree and NZDF case studies; About page. Read the source page

  4. 04

    How the part is designed

    Most of the savings customers describe came from changing the design, not the price per hour. Pertronic formed hinges into the cabinet body and replaced press-inserted studs with formed threaded holes: one machine operation instead of two. Dyno’s assembly went from nine parts to five and from half an hour to two to five minutes. Jackbord’s frame brackets were bent 20 at a time. Pure Audio used chassis offcuts as heat-sinks.

    Pertronic, Dyno, Jackbord and Pure Audio case studies. Read the source page

  5. 05

    Finish

    Hand-finishing was “too laborious, inconsistent, and expensive” for Designtree, and Pure Audio found the same: a hand finish would not be consistent and the labour “would make it uneconomical”. Metco changed how those parts were cut and machined instead. Powder coating, on the other hand, is done in-house: up to 24 m long, in Dulux colours, with colour matching for high volume runs. Piazza was advised to replace a powder-coated base with zinc coating or stainless steel, and Omeo to use stainless steel instead of powder coating on unseen parts.

    Designtree, Pure Audio, Piazza and Omeo case studies; powder coating page. Read the source page

  6. 06

    What is in the quote

    Dyno says of Metco: “Everything is quoted for so we know where we stand and there’s no hidden or surprise costs.” Metco also folds, welds, powder coats, assembles, packs and delivers in-house, which Dyno credits with keeping handling and time down: “Their efficient handling keeps time and costs down.” Ask for the whole job quoted at once.

    Dyno case study. Read the source page

Questions buyers ask

Does Metco publish laser cutting prices?

No. There is no price list on its site. The contact page offers a free quote by phone, email or form, and a consultation that covers pricing.

Can I get a one-off or small run laser cut?

Metco says it supplies over 600 clients with work “ranging from one-off commissions to contract runs of tens of thousands of units”. Designtree orders 10 to 20 items at a time, and the NZ Defence Force reports no restrictions on minimum quantities.

What is the thickest steel the laser can cut?

Mild steel up to 25 mm, stainless steel up to 20 mm, aluminium up to 16 mm, copper and brass up to 8 mm, on a working range of 3050 mm × 1550 mm.

Do I need a finished drawing before I ask?

No. Metco’s process page says you do not need all the answers. Its designers work in SolidWorks, JetCAM and GibbsCAM, and it offers a free consultation to take an idea to a drawing. A drawing does make a quote faster to prepare.

Can Metco finish and assemble the parts too?

Yes. Metco sands, rumbles and buffs, powder coats (up to 24 m long), assembles, packages and transports, all in-house.

Where does Metco deliver?

Wellington, Auckland, Christchurch and across New Zealand, and “anywhere within New Zealand or the world”. There are two offices: Lower Hutt and Eden Terrace, Auckland.
